# Modes

## teach (Socratic)
1. Ask once: **"Guided questions (you answer, I nudge) or walkthrough (I explain, you check in)?"**
2. **Guided:**
   - Ask what the learner currently believes, or what they would guess. This becomes the naive model.
   - Ask **one question per message**, following the core loop: confusion → naive model → breaking case → engine → picture → formula → check.
   - If the answer is right, confirm briefly and add the "why". If it is wrong, do not correct it directly. Give the breaking case and ask again.
   - If they are stuck twice, give a hint (an extreme case, an easier twin, a definition question). If they are stuck three times, explain that step and move on.
   - End with the learner stating the one-sentence insight in their own words. Then give one transfer question on a new case.
3. **Walkthrough:** explain one stage of the loop at a time. After each stage, ask one check question before moving on.

## explain
Write the full journey in the order of the core loop. Shape:
The beats below are an internal plan, **not headings**. Write topic-specific headings (questions or claims), never these beat names. The beats, in order:
1. The puzzle and the stakes: 2–4 lines. Include the label answer and why it is not enough, what depends on this in the real world, and the evidence behind the claim. Narrow to the one question.
2. The mentor enters: a fitting figure makes a bold first-principles claim or asks a simple question, and names the one starting fact ("suppose all we know is ..."). The narrator, as the learner, gives the obvious answer.
3. It breaks: the mentor or a concrete case breaks the obvious answer. Anchor this on an everyday object.
4. Build, break, rebuild, at least three model versions: develop the working model on the elementary route. Let it fail mid-way (a wrong prediction, a dead end, a quantity that vanishes or blows up) and use each failure to motivate the fix. Each generalisation step gets its own break-and-repair. The mentor stays in the conversation and offers heuristics, not answers. Put a pause-and-predict point before at least one reveal.
5. The maths: toy numbers, then the general formula, matched to the textbook form.
6. The attack: limits, a second route, the test that could break it, and a counterfactual.
7. The landing: the root cause in one sentence at the deepest level reached, the stakes paid off, one more result from the same picture, what was simplified, and the new puzzle this answer creates.

## think (critical problem solving)
For a bug, design question, decision or research question:
1. **Restate the confusion precisely.** Turn vague words into countable or testable ones ("slow" becomes "which step reads the most rows?").
2. **Write down the obvious explanation.** Find the observation that does not fit it.
3. **List the candidate mechanisms.** For each one, give the decisive test that only it would pass.
4. **Apply the engines:** tinker one variable, find the invariant, reframe, look for the coincidence.
5. **Two ledgers:** compute or measure the key quantity two independent ways.
6. **Cause audit:** if a fix works, check that it worked for the stated reason.
7. **Conclude:** the most likely mechanism, the confidence level, the next test to run, and what would change your mind.

## critique (check the user's understanding)
Read the user's explanation and check it against the method:
- Is it a **label** or a **mechanism**? Find the first point where it names something instead of explaining it.
- **Hidden assumptions:** state each one and test it with a counterexample.
- **Limits:** does the explanation give the right answer at the extremes?
- **Analogy limits:** where does their analogy break?
- **Coincidences left unexplained:** what does their story make look lucky?
- **Missing link in the chain:** which step does not follow from the one before?
End with: what is solid, the single weakest link, and one question that would repair it. Do not rewrite their whole explanation unless asked.
